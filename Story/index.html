02 / THE OVERVIEW

Before You Begin

Hong Kong’s government has promised a two-year “30,000 Youth Employment and Internship Programme” in the 2026 Policy Address. It aims to give young people with little or no work experience practical exposure and a clearer path into the workforce, with one major strand due to launch in the first quarter of 2027. Yet a long-standing statutory exemption under the Minimum Wage Ordinance allows full-time students under 26 to work for up to 59 continuous days outside the minimum-wage floor. The placement need not be arranged by a university and need not relate to the student’s course. Employers can therefore label routine operational roles as internships and pay little or nothing.

This investigation, based on internship advertisements, interviews with three students, two human-resources officers, a lawyer and the Labour Department, plus government and audit data, finds that many current placements already operate in this grey zone. A substantial share of postings offer no allowance. Where pay is stated, it frequently sits well below the statutory rate that will rise to $43.10 an hour in May 2026. After transport and meal costs, the three interviewed students’ effective hourly returns ranged from roughly $17 to the low forties, or turned negative for the unpaid placement. One student judged $5,000 a month sufficient; another found $7,200 inadequate for a complex solo project; the unpaid student concluded that high personal costs outweighed the feedback received. These are not isolated anecdotes. A 2021 Society for Community Organisation survey of low-income students showed a clear gap between the allowance they expected and what many actually received, confirming that unpaid or low-paid work functions as a financial filter.

Past public schemes have already shown weak delivery and almost no workplace inspections. The Audit Commission recorded shortfalls in training hours and filled places, and zero site visits to certain workplaces over several years. Employer interviews described interns as a future talent pipeline tempered by budget pressure, or simply “no work, no pay.” The new programme will layer subsidies of up to $11,000 per intern onto the same exemption without introducing training standards, inspection requirements or outcome reporting.

The story matters because public money is about to expand a system that already converts education-labelled placements into low-cost labour while shifting the real costs onto students. Those who can absorb transport, meals and foregone wages gain the CV advantage; those who cannot are screened out before they start. Similar patterns appear in UK graduate surveys and EU traineeship data, where unpaid or poorly regulated placements widen class gaps in access. If the exemption remains untouched, the 30,000 places risk subsidising the existing inequality rather than closing it. The question is no longer whether Hong Kong needs more internships, but whether the rules beneath them will deliver training and mobility—or simply more cheap labour.

01 / THE FINANCIAL COST

When Travel and Meals Eat Into Your Pay

Intern A — Media Student (City University of Hong Kong)

Intern A completed a full-time journalism placement found through the university platform. It was an elective linked to the degree. The student worked Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 7 p.m., with no overtime. Daily transport and meal costs were about HK$90.

What they put in: Time on social-media tasks that matched the job description, plus everyday expenses that reduced the monthly HK$5,000 allowance.

What they gained: Feedback on every article from colleagues, a lighter workload than expected, improved writing skills, chances to attend events, a recommendation letter, and an offer to continue as a freelancer in a different role.

How they evaluated it: The salary covered basic living costs. The effort felt balanced by the practical experience and fulfilled promises. Overall, Intern A was satisfied.

Key point: Even a positive internship can shrink in financial value once daily costs are counted. Estimated cash remaining after expenses: approximately HK$17.20 per hour.

02 / WORKING WITHOUT PAY

Working Without Pay

Intern B — Psychology Student (The Education University of Hong Kong)

Intern B took a compulsory unpaid placement arranged through the university as a course requirement. It lasted about 140 hours over three months and involved board-game tutoring, including work with students with special educational needs (SEN).

What they put in: Full workdays of around nine hours, increasing administrative tasks during the placement, and more than HK$1,000 in transport and meal costs for travel to schools across Hong Kong. The student received no allowance.

What they gained: First-hand practice leading activities, observing experienced tutors, adapting teaching methods for teachers, adults, primary school pupils and SEN students, as well as feedback from colleagues. The work was connected to behavioural psychology.

How they evaluated it: The meaningful experience and feedback provided real learning value, but the lack of pay reduced the student's motivation. Intern B also received a B- in a mid-term course evaluation and felt pressure to demonstrate professional knowledge while effectively paying to work. Balancing these costs and benefits was difficult.

Key point: When an internship is required for academic credit, students may still have to cover the costs themselves. The estimated financial return after expenses was negative.

03 / BEYOND SALARY

Beyond Salary

Intern C — Business Systems Student (City University of Hong Kong)

Intern C completed a mandatory summer internship in a logistics company's finance department, found through a university course partnership. The placement lasted 38 working days, around 304 hours, with a monthly salary of HK$7,200. Commuting took approximately 90 minutes each way.

What they put in: A large independent project to renew and improve an existing finance system using an updated programming language. The project was bigger and more complicated than expected, alongside the time and cost of commuting.

What they gained: Daily morning check-ins with the supervisor, step-by-step guidance when problems arose, company orientation activities, deeper practical coding experience, experience managing a full project and career advice from colleagues.

How they evaluated it: The student felt the salary was unreasonable for the level of responsibility and believed more guidance at the start would have helped. Nevertheless, Intern C valued the organised workplace, professional connections, industry insight and personal growth that could not be taught in class. Overall, the experience was positive.

"The job I was in charge of was too complicated for an intern to work on."

Key point: Higher pay does not automatically resolve questions about internship quality. Estimated cash remaining after expenses: approximately HK$35.90 per hour.

Financial figures are estimates based on interview information and calculation assumptions. They are not statutory hourly wages.

Students describe what they contribute and what they gain. But how do employers approach internship recruitment, compensation and training?

04 / THE EMPLOYER PERSPECTIVE

Building a Future Talent Pipeline

Employer A — Human Resources, Public Relations Agency

A human resources officer at a small-to-medium-sized public relations agency described hiring interns as a way of "building a future talent pipeline".

"Budget is a real consideration."

The employer explained that training new staff requires time and effort, especially for a lean team. Interns are assigned mentors and given opportunities to gain practical experience. The agency aims to balance operational needs with learning and professional development.

04 / THE EMPLOYER PERSPECTIVE

No Work, No Pay?

Employer B — Human Resources, Insurance Company

Employer B described a different payment arrangement. Interns are paid on commission rather than receiving a fixed monthly allowance.

"No work = no pay."

According to the employer, interns are not expected to make significant contributions to daily operations. The company also described training related to insurance products, licensing and communication skills, with the possibility of moving into a longer-term role.

The employer said the arrangements are explained when the contract is signed. However, commission-based payments can make income less predictable for students.

Key point: The two employers described different ways of balancing recruitment needs, limited resources, training and pay. Students must consider whether the opportunities offered match their own expectations.

When does a disappointing internship become a legal issue?

05 / THE LEGAL BOUNDARIES

Legal Boundaries

Hong Kong's Minimum Wage Ordinance provides separate exemption arrangements for eligible student interns and work experience students.

A student intern may qualify for an exemption when the placement forms a compulsory or elective part of an eligible education programme and is arranged or endorsed by the educational institution.

Separately, eligible full-time work experience students under 26 may agree to an exemption period of up to 59 consecutive days if the required statutory procedures are completed.

The statutory minimum wage has been HK$43.10 per hour since 1 May 2026. Whether it applies to a particular internship depends on the legal status of the placement and whether the exemption criteria are satisfied.

In our interview, a lawyer explained that assigning routine tasks, or work unrelated to a student's studies, does not automatically constitute a legal violation. Similarly, a lack of promised mentorship may be disappointing without necessarily creating a legal claim. The specific agreement and circumstances matter.

Key point: An internship may meet legal requirements while still raising questions about financial return and learning quality. These are different issues.

What should students check?

Ask about pay, daily duties, supervision, training expectations and whether the placement qualifies for academic credit.

How does the Labour Department explain the rules and the protection available to students?

D / LABOUR DEPARTMENT

The Official Response

In its written response to our reporting team, the Labour Department explained that the student exemption arrangements aim to balance the protection of student employees' rights with opportunities to gain internship and work experience.

The Department referred to the conditions under the Minimum Wage Ordinance and directed us to its official guidance for student employees and employers.

The Department also described the newly announced "30 000 Youth Employment and Internship Programme", which covers several employment and training initiatives. The programme includes full-time employment measures, rather than consisting entirely of university internships.

The Department did not provide the requested complaint or inspection figures in its response. This limited what the reporting team could independently assess about enforcement and oversight.

05 / THE BIGGER PICTURE

What Did We Learn?

Across our three student interviews, pay levels and personal evaluations differed. Internship value was shaped not only by money, but also by workload, training, mentorship and future career opportunities.

01 / FINANCIAL RETURN

Pay Is Not the Whole Picture

Transport, meals and other expenses can reduce the cash students keep from their internships. For unpaid interns, these costs may mean spending money to work.

02 / WORKLOAD

Responsibility Matters

Even a relatively higher salary may feel insufficient when an intern takes on complex responsibilities. Workload and working conditions affect how students evaluate their experience.

03 / LEARNING & MENTORSHIP

Learning Can Change the Experience

Guidance, feedback and meaningful tasks can make an internship valuable, even when the allowance is low. But students may not receive the same quality of support.

07 / BACK TO THE QUESTION

What Makes an Internship Worth It?

Our investigation shows that the value of an internship cannot be measured by salary alone. The three students we interviewed had different experiences: one found a low-paid internship worthwhile, another struggled with the costs of unpaid work, while a higher-paid intern still felt that the salary did not reflect the responsibilities involved.

What makes an internship worthwhile is the relationship between what students contribute and what they receive in return. Pay and expenses matter, but so do meaningful responsibilities, guidance, practical skills and future career opportunities. Before accepting an offer, students should have a clearer understanding of both the financial costs and the learning opportunities available to them.

“It's hard to balance between what I have invested and the knowledge I've gained.”

Intern B — Unpaid psychology internship
